Why we exist

Power that is never reviewed does not correct itself.

Most criminal cases never reach a jury. They are resolved by charging decisions, plea leverage, and whether a person can afford to go home before trial. Those are the moments that determine outcomes, and they attract the least public scrutiny.

We work in public: we explain how the system actually operates, publish what our research finds, and advocate for reforms that do not make justice a function of wealth, status, or institutional power.

Our mission

Fairness, accountability, transparency, and equal treatment

Hold Prosecutors Accountable, Inc. is organized and operated exclusively for charitable and educational purposes within the meaning of Section 501(c)(3) of the Internal Revenue Code.

Purpose

The organization's purpose is to promote fairness, accountability, transparency, and equal treatment within the criminal justice system; educate the public concerning prosecutorial authority and accountability, criminal justice policy, the rights of accused and formerly incarcerated individuals, and barriers to successful reentry; conduct and disseminate research concerning criminal justice practices and potential reforms; and promote policies and practices designed to ensure that access to justice and opportunities for successful reentry are not determined by wealth, status, or institutional power.

The system we are working toward

I

Government officials are appropriately accountable for misconduct.

II

Accused individuals have meaningful access to a fair defense regardless of financial resources.

III

Pretrial detention decisions are based on individualized judicial determinations rather than an individual's ability to pay.

IV

People who have completed criminal sentences have a meaningful opportunity to reintegrate into society.

Pillar one

Prosecutorial accountability

The power to charge is the power to decide an outcome. It should be exercised in public view, and it should carry consequences when it is abused.

A prosecutor decides whether to bring a case, which charges to file, what to offer in a plea, and what to disclose to the defense. Each of those choices shapes a person's life more than a verdict does, and most of them are never reviewed by a court, a board, or a voter with the information needed to judge them.

We work to make that authority legible to the public, and to build the case for accountability when it is misused.

Educate the public on how the authority works

Charging discretion, plea leverage, disclosure obligations, and immunity doctrines, explained without jargon so that people can follow what happens in their own courthouse.

Document patterns, not just incidents

A single reversal is a story. A repeated practice is a policy. Our research looks for the practices that recur across cases and offices.

Support meaningful review

We promote policies and practices designed to ensure that misconduct is examined by someone with the authority to act on it.

Report misconduct

If you experienced or witnessed prosecutorial misconduct, you can tell us about it. We are not a law firm and cannot represent you, but what you send informs our research and our public education work.

Pillar two

The right to a fair defense

A defense that depends on a bank balance is not a right. Neither is freedom before trial that depends on a deposit.

Defense regardless of financial resources

The constitutional guarantee of counsel is only as real as the time, investigation, and expert help behind it. When a defense is under-resourced, the outcome is decided by capacity rather than by facts. We educate the public about what adequate defense requires and advocate for the conditions that make it possible.

Pretrial detention decided by a judge, not a price

Pretrial detention decisions should be based on individualized judicial determinations rather than an individual's ability to pay. Detention before trial costs people their jobs, their housing, and their custody arrangements, and it increases the pressure to plead guilty regardless of guilt.

What we publish

Plain-language explanations of arraignment, bail hearings, conditions of release, and what to ask your attorney at each stage.

What we advocate for

Release decisions grounded in individualized findings, and defense systems resourced to do the work the Constitution assumes.

If you or someone you love is being held before trial, our Get Help page lists the questions to ask and where to turn. It is free, and it is written for people in the middle of a case.

Pillar three

Housing, banking, and life after release

A completed sentence should end the punishment. Too often it begins a second one, administered by landlords and banks.

A person leaves custody having served their sentence, and then encounters a background check on every apartment application and a closed door at the bank. Without a home and an account, work is harder to keep and stability is harder to build, which makes every other outcome worse.

We educate the public about the barriers and discrimination faced by formerly incarcerated individuals, and we advocate, within the limitations applicable to organizations exempt under Section 501(c)(3), for laws and policies that would establish formerly incarcerated individuals as a legally protected class with respect to housing and banking, and prohibit unjustified discrimination in those areas based solely on an individual's prior incarceration.

Housing

A record is not a reason on its own

Blanket exclusions based solely on prior incarceration deny housing to people who present no risk at all. We advocate for protected-class status and for individualized assessment in its place.

Banking

Access to an account is access to everything

Wages, rent, and benefits move through bank accounts. Refusing one based solely on prior incarceration pushes people out of the financial system entirely.

I am facing charges

What happens at each stage, and what to ask your attorney.

→

A criminal case moves through a set of predictable stages: arrest or summons, arraignment, discovery, pretrial motions, plea negotiation, and — rarely — trial. Most cases end in a plea, so the decisions made long before any trial date are the ones that matter most.

Questions worth asking your attorney, in writing if you can:

  • What exactly am I charged with, and what is the sentencing exposure on each count?
  • What has the prosecution turned over in discovery, and what is still outstanding?
  • Are there motions to suppress evidence or to dismiss any count?
  • What plea has been offered, when does it expire, and what happens if I decline it?
  • What are the consequences beyond the sentence — immigration, housing, licensing, employment?

General information only, not legal advice. If you do not have a lawyer, contact your local public defender's office immediately — deadlines in criminal cases are short.

I am being held before trial

Bail hearings, conditions of release, and how to seek review.

→

Detention before trial is supposed to rest on an individualized judicial finding about flight risk and safety — not on whether a person can post a bond. In practice, the amount set often decides the outcome.

  • Ask your attorney what findings the judge made on the record, and whether they were individualized.
  • Ask whether non-financial conditions (supervision, check-ins, electronic monitoring) were considered and rejected, and why.
  • Ask whether a bail review, reconsideration motion, or appeal is available, and on what timeline.
  • Gather what supports release: employment, housing, caregiving responsibilities, treatment enrollment, community ties.

General information only, not legal advice. Procedures and deadlines differ by state and by court.

I was denied housing because of a record

What the denial can and cannot be based on, and what to document.

→

Many denials rely on a background check run by a consumer reporting agency. When that is what happened, federal fair-credit rules generally entitle you to notice of the denial, the name of the agency that supplied the report, and a copy of the report so you can dispute what is wrong in it.

  • Ask for the denial in writing, including the specific reason.
  • Request a copy of the background report and check it for errors: wrong person, dismissed charges, sealed or expunged records, duplicate entries.
  • Dispute inaccuracies with the reporting agency in writing and keep copies.
  • Save the listing, the application, the fee receipt, and every message with the landlord or management company.
  • Check whether your state or city limits how criminal history may be used in housing decisions — several do.

General information only, not legal advice. A local legal aid office or fair-housing organization can tell you what applies where you live.

A bank refused me an account

Why it happens, what to ask for in writing, and where to complain.

→

Account denials are often driven by a banking-history database rather than by a criminal record itself — an old unpaid overdraft can follow someone for years. Either way, you are entitled to know which report was used.

  • Ask the bank for the reason in writing and for the name of the reporting agency it used.
  • Request your file from that agency and dispute anything inaccurate.
  • Ask whether the bank offers a second-chance or basic checking account.
  • Credit unions and community development financial institutions often use different criteria than large banks.
  • Complaints about a bank's conduct can be filed with the Consumer Financial Protection Bureau and with your state banking regulator.

General information only, not legal advice.

I want to report prosecutorial misconduct

What to send us, and what we can and cannot do with it.

→

We are a research and public education organization. We cannot represent you, file anything on your behalf, or intervene in a pending case. What you send helps us document patterns and informs what we publish and advocate for.

If you choose to write to us, the most useful account includes:

  • The county and court, and the case number if you have it.
  • What happened, in your own words, and roughly when.
  • Whether the issue was raised in court, and what the court did.
  • Any documents you already have — transcripts, motions, orders, correspondence.
  • Whether you are represented, and whether you want us to keep your name out of anything we publish.

Do not send originals, and do not send anything covered by attorney–client privilege without talking to your lawyer first. Contacting us does not create an attorney–client relationship, and nothing you send is privileged.
